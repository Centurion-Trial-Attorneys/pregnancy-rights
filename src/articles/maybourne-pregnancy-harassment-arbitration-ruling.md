<div class="tldr" markdown="1">
**The short version:** On November 17, 2026, a California appeals court ruled that a former Beverly Hills hotel manager can take her pregnancy harassment case to court even though she signed an arbitration agreement. The court held that the federal law against forced arbitration of "sexual harassment" disputes also covers harassment because of pregnancy, and it kept her entire case in court. No court has decided whether her allegations are true.
</div>

The case is *Jane Doe v. The Maybourne Beverly Hills Operator LLC* (Cal. Ct. App., 2d Dist., Div. 7, No. B341400). The opinion is [certified for publication](https://news.workcompacademy.com/2026/Doe-v-The-Maybourne-Beverly-Hills-Operator-LLC-B341400.PDF), so California trial courts have to follow it. Law360 and [HCAmag](https://www.hcamag.com/us/specialization/employment-law/pregnancy-harassment-dodges-the-fine-print-at-beverly-hills-hotel/598100) also reported the ruling.

## What does the lawsuit allege?

The worker, who sued under the name Jane Doe, was a guest reception manager at The Maybourne Beverly Hills from May 2015 until January 2024. In June 2020 she signed an agreement to send employment disputes to private arbitration. Here is what her complaint alleges, as the appeals court described it:

- After she told the hotel she was pregnant in October 2020, she was told she "would not be able to be a career woman once she had children."
- After her maternity leave, she applied for a director job. She was never interviewed, and the job went to a man.
- In December 2023, a regional director told her "that she was a mother and that her first priority would always be her children." HCAmag reports she was told there was "no point in asking" when she pressed for fair pay.
- She was then accused of a sexual relationship with a coworker, which she says was false. She was suspended without pay and fired five days later.

She sued in March 2024 under California's Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA) for a hostile work environment based on sex, gender and pregnancy, along with sex discrimination, retaliation and wrongful termination. These are allegations, and the hotel hasn't had its day in court on them.

## What did the hotel argue?

The hotel asked the court to send the case to arbitration. It argued that the federal Ending Forced Arbitration of Sexual Assault and Sexual Harassment Act (EFAA) only covers harassment that is "sexual" in nature, not harassment about gender or pregnancy. It also argued she alleged "[a]t most ... two comments," and that being passed over for promotion is a discrimination claim, not harassment. Counsel for the hotel didn't immediately respond to Law360's request for comment.

## What did the court decide?

A three-judge panel affirmed the Los Angeles Superior Court's order keeping the case in court. Justice Natalie P. Stone wrote that "a 'sexual harassment dispute' under the EFAA can include a FEHA hostile workplace claim based on harassment due to gender or pregnancy, without any lascivious acts, advances, or comments."

The court's reasoning was simple. The EFAA tells judges to use the definition of sexual harassment in whatever law the worker sues under. FEHA says harassment because of sex "includes sexual harassment, gender harassment, and harassment based on pregnancy, childbirth, or related medical conditions," and that "[s]exually harassing conduct need not be motivated by sexual desire" (Gov. Code § 12940(j)(4)(C)). The court said the law's #MeToo origins don't override its plain words, pointing to the U.S. Supreme Court's reasoning in *Bostock v. Clayton County*.

Because she adequately alleged a harassment dispute, the court kept her entire case in court, including the discrimination and retaliation claims. The court was careful to say it wasn't deciding who is right: "A trier of fact ultimately may conclude the conduct was not 'sufficiently severe or pervasive.'"

## What happens next?

The case goes back to the trial court. The hotel could still ask the California Supreme Court to review the decision.

## What this means for California workers

If you signed an arbitration agreement at work, this ruling matters.

- **Comments about your pregnancy can be harassment.** Under FEHA, harassment because of sex includes harassment based on pregnancy, when it's severe or pervasive enough. It doesn't have to be sexual, and it doesn't have to come from someone who's attracted to you.
- **You may be able to choose court over arbitration.** The EFAA lets you decide. A pregnancy harassment claim can now trigger that choice in California, even if you signed the agreement years ago. The law covers disputes that arose on or after March 3, 2022.
- **It can bring your other claims along.** Here, the whole case stayed in court, not just the harassment claim.
- **Harassment is the key.** The ruling turned on her harassment claim. A case about a pregnancy firing or demotion with no harassment allegations wasn't before this court.

If you're hearing comments like these, write them down: the exact words, who said them, the date, and who else was there.
