An assisted living facility in Blue Island, Illinois, has agreed to pay $160,000 to settle a federal lawsuit claiming it fired a pregnant certified nursing assistant instead of accommodating her lifting restriction, the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) [announced on November 17](https://www.eeoc.gov/newsroom/prairie-green-fays-point-pay-160000-pregnancy-discrimination). It's the second settlement of an EEOC Pregnant Workers Fairness Act (PWFA) case in a week, after [Wabash National settled the agency's first PWFA lawsuit](/articles/wabash-national-eeoc-pwfa-settlement/).

## What did the EEOC allege?

According to the EEOC, Blue Island SLF, LLC, which does business as Prairie Green at Fay's Point, fired a certified nursing assistant because of a pregnancy-related lifting restriction. The agency says the facility told her lifting was an essential function of her job and that her only option was unpaid leave for the rest of her pregnancy. When she declined, she was fired. The EEOC alleges that other vacant jobs were available that she could have done temporarily.

The [Daily Southtown and Chicago Tribune reported](https://www.yahoo.com/news/us/articles/blue-island-assisted-living-facility-195800369.html), based on the complaint, that she gave the facility a doctor's note with a 20-pound lifting restriction in 2023. For a time, the facility informally accommodated her by having another staff member swap residents with her when lifting was needed. According to the complaint, the facility later denied her formal accommodation request, fired her in August 2023, and less than a month later posted a concierge opening that it didn't offer her.

These are the EEOC's allegations. The settlement isn't a court finding that the facility broke the law, and neither the EEOC's announcement nor news coverage reports that the company admitted wrongdoing. Prairie Green "did not immediately respond" to requests for comment, according to the Daily Southtown. We are not naming the worker.

The EEOC filed the case in September 2025 in federal court in the Northern District of Illinois (*EEOC v. Blue Island SLF, LLC d/b/a Prairie Green at Fay's Point*, No. 1:25-cv-11111 (N.D. Ill.)), after it said it first tried to resolve the matter without a lawsuit. The agency [filed it the same day](https://www.eeoc.gov/index%2Ephp/newsroom/eeoc-sues-two-businesses-operating-chicago-area-under-pregnant-workers-fairness-act) as a second PWFA suit against R&L Carriers, which the EEOC alleges forced a pregnant truck driver onto leave. In both cases, the agency says, the worker asked for an accommodation for a 20-pound lifting restriction that would have let her keep working, and the employer refused. The R&L Carriers case is still pending.

## What does the settlement require?

According to the EEOC, Prairie Green will:

- pay $160,000 in lost wages to the former employee;
- review and revise its pregnancy accommodation policies;
- write guidelines on possible accommodations for jobs with lifting requirements; and
- give its employees annual training on the PWFA.

"Employers cannot simply force pregnant workers onto unpaid leave when reasonable accommodations are available," EEOC Chair Andrea R. Lucas said in the announcement.

## Why does this case matter?

When the EEOC filed this suit, agency spokesperson Victor Chen said the law "specifically bars employers from forcing employees to take leave when the employer could have offered another reasonable accommodation that would allow them to keep working." He added: "It was designed to prevent what happened here: pregnant workers being pushed out of the workforce."

The fact pattern is a common one. A worker in a physical job gets a lifting restriction. The employer calls lifting "essential" and offers unpaid leave as the only answer. Meanwhile, other work she could do sits open. This settlement shows the EEOC is still enforcing the PWFA against that pattern, even as the agency [weighs narrowing its PWFA rules](/articles/eeoc-pregnant-workers-fairness-act-rule-rollback/).

## What this means for California workers

The PWFA applies in California, and it bars an employer from taking adverse action against you "on account of the employee requesting or using a reasonable accommodation" for pregnancy-related limitations (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg-1(5)). It also prohibits retaliation against anyone who opposes a PWFA violation or files a charge (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg-2(f)(1)).

California law covers the same ground, and in some ways more of it:

- **Accommodation and transfer.** California's pregnancy law requires reasonable accommodation and gives you the right to ask for a temporary transfer to a less strenuous or hazardous position (Gov. Code § 12945(a)(3)). Our guide on [how to ask for a pregnancy accommodation](/articles/how-to-ask-for-pregnancy-accommodation-california/) includes a sample request.
- **No interference or retaliation.** Your employer can't deny, interfere with, or restrain your rights to accommodation, transfer, or pregnancy disability leave, including by retaliating against you for using them (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11039(a)(2)(F)).
- **No firing because of pregnancy.** It's unlawful to discharge an employee because of pregnancy or perceived pregnancy (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11039(a)(1)(D)).
- **Leave is your call.** If your employer is pushing unpaid leave on you when you can still work, read our guide on [whether your employer can make you take pregnancy leave](/articles/employer-force-pregnancy-leave-california/).

If your employer says "there's nothing we can do" about a lifting restriction, ask it to name the duties it's worried about and what other work is open. Put your request in writing, keep your doctor's note, and save every response. You generally have three years to file a complaint with the California Civil Rights Department (Gov. Code § 12960(e)(5)).
