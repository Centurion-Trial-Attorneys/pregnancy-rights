<div class="tldr" markdown="1">
**The short version:** California law already requires most employers to accommodate pregnancy, and it covers businesses with as few as five employees. If your employer has 15 or more employees, the federal Pregnant Workers Fairness Act (PWFA) stacks extra protections on top: limits on demands for doctor's notes, protection when you temporarily can't do part of your job, a ban on forcing you onto leave, and a second agency you can file with. You can rely on both laws at once.
</div>

California's pregnancy protections are strong, and for many workers they reach further than federal law. Still, the PWFA fills some gaps and gives you tools state law doesn't spell out. Here's what it adds and how to use it.

## Who does the PWFA cover?

The PWFA took effect June 27, 2023. It applies to employers with 15 or more employees, including government employers (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg(2)(B)). It protects employees and job applicants (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg(6)).

California's pregnancy accommodation and leave rules apply to employers with five or more employees (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11035(h)). So if you work for a business with five to 14 people, California law is your protection. At 15 or more, both laws apply. Your employer has to follow both, so in practice the more protective rule wins.

## What does the PWFA require my employer to do?

A covered employer has to make reasonable accommodations for the "known limitations" of a qualified employee related to pregnancy, childbirth, or related medical conditions, unless the accommodation would cause an undue hardship (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg-1(1)).

A "known limitation" is a physical or mental condition related to pregnancy or childbirth that you or someone on your behalf has told your employer about. It doesn't have to be serious enough to count as a disability under the Americans with Disabilities Act (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg(4)). Nausea, back pain, a lifting limit, and needing time for prenatal appointments can all qualify.

California has a similar duty. Your employer can't refuse a reasonable accommodation for a pregnancy-related condition when you ask for it on your health care provider's advice (Gov. Code § 12945(a)(3)(A); Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11040(a)(1)). For a step-by-step walkthrough, see our guide on [how to ask for a pregnancy accommodation](/articles/how-to-ask-for-pregnancy-accommodation-california/).

## Does my employer need a doctor's note for everything?

Under the PWFA, often no.

California's regulations let an employer ask for a written medical certification before granting an accommodation or transfer (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11050(b)). The federal rules are tighter. An employer covered by the PWFA may ask for supporting documentation only when it's reasonable to do so (29 C.F.R. § 1636.3(l)(1)).

The EEOC has named four requests that it says will be reasonable accommodations "in virtually all cases" (29 C.F.R. § 1636.3(j)(4)):

- Keeping water nearby and drinking as needed
- Taking extra restroom breaks as needed
- Sitting if your job requires standing, or standing if it requires sitting
- Taking breaks to eat and drink as needed

If you're pregnant and ask for one of these, your own simple statement that you need it is enough. Your employer isn't supposed to demand a note (29 C.F.R. § 1636.3(l)(1)(iii)). The same goes when your need and the fix are obvious, when your employer already has enough information, and for many pumping requests (29 C.F.R. § 1636.3(l)(1)).

When documentation is reasonable, your employer can ask only for the minimum needed, can't insist on its own form, and can't make you see a doctor it picks (29 C.F.R. § 1636.3(l)(2), (l)(3)).

## What if I can't do part of my job for a while?

The PWFA protects you here too. Under the statute, you still count as "qualified" even if you can't perform an essential function of your job, as long as the inability is temporary, you could do it again in the near future, and the inability can be reasonably accommodated (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg(6)).

In practice, your employer may have to set aside a task for a while, such as heavy lifting or climbing ladders, instead of telling you that you can't do the job. California also lets you ask for a temporary transfer to a less strenuous or hazardous position when your provider advises it (Gov. Code § 12945(a)(3)(C)).

## Can my employer put me on leave instead?

Not if something else would work. The PWFA bars an employer from requiring you to take leave, paid or unpaid, if another reasonable accommodation can be provided (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg-1(4)).

The law also bars an employer from:

- Forcing you to accept an accommodation that wasn't worked out with you through the interactive process (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg-1(2))
- Denying you a job or opportunity because you'd need an accommodation (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg-1(3))
- Punishing you for asking for or using an accommodation (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg-1(5))
- Threatening, coercing, or interfering with you for exercising these rights (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg-2(f))

Forced leave matters because it can drain time you may need later. In California, an accommodation doesn't use up your four months of Pregnancy Disability Leave (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11040(b)). Our overview of [pregnancy rights at work in California](/articles/pregnancy-rights-at-work-california/) explains how that leave works.

## Where do I file if my employer breaks the PWFA?

The PWFA is enforced through the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC), using the same procedures and remedies as Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg-2(a)(1)). In California, you generally have 300 days from the date of the harm to file a charge with the EEOC, according to the [EEOC's Los Angeles office](https://www.eeoc.gov/field-office/losangeles/timeliness).

Claims under California law go to the California Civil Rights Department, and you generally have three years to file there (Gov. Code § 12960(e)(5)). Don't let the shorter federal clock run out while you wait.

## Is the PWFA changing?

Parts of the federal rules are in flux. In May 2025, a federal court in Louisiana vacated the parts of the EEOC's PWFA rule that required employers to accommodate elective abortions. The EEOC's regulatory agenda also lists a proposal, targeted for November 2026, to revise how the agency interprets "pregnancy, childbirth, or related medical conditions."

The core of the law is written into the statute and doesn't depend on the EEOC's rule: the duty to accommodate, the ban on forced leave, and the ban on retaliation. California's own protections don't change either way.

## What should I do now?

- **Count heads.** Find out roughly how many people your employer has. Five or more means California law applies. Fifteen or more adds the PWFA.
- **Ask in writing.** Email your manager or HR and say what you need because of your pregnancy. Plain words are fine.
- **Push back on unnecessary notes.** If you asked for water, restroom breaks, food breaks, or a chair, and your employer has 15 or more employees, you can point out that federal rules generally don't allow it to demand a doctor's note for those requests.
- **Don't accept forced leave quietly.** If your employer sends you home when another fix would work, put your objection in writing and keep a copy.
- **Watch the 300-day deadline.** If something goes wrong, get help well before that window closes. If you've already been let go, start with our guide on [what to do in the first week after being fired while pregnant](/articles/fired-while-pregnant-first-week/).
