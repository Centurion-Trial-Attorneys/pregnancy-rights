Semi-trailer maker Wabash National Corp. has agreed to pay $50,000 and change its policies nationwide to settle the first lawsuit the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) ever filed under the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act (PWFA), the agency [announced on November 13](https://www.eeoc.gov/newsroom/wabash-national-settles-eeoc-pregnancy-discrimination-lawsuit). The EEOC alleged the company denied a pregnant assembler's request to move off a job that required her to lie on her stomach, put her on unpaid leave instead, and left her no real choice but to quit.

## What did the EEOC allege?

According to the EEOC, a pregnant employee at Wabash's plant in Cadiz, Kentucky, asked to transfer to a position that didn't require lying on her stomach. The EEOC says the company denied the request, forced her onto unpaid leave, and then gave her one option: come back to her assembler job with no changes.

The agency alleges that the denial left her afraid for the health of her pregnancy, and that she was forced to resign in November 2023, when she was nearly eight months pregnant. The EEOC also alleged that Wabash unlawfully required medical documentation and failed to make changes it could have made for non-pregnant workers with similar limitations. The suit claimed violations of the PWFA, Title VII of the Civil Rights Act, and the Americans with Disabilities Act.

These are the EEOC's allegations. No court made findings on them. The EEOC did not name the worker.

The EEOC filed the case in September 2024 in federal court in Kentucky (*EEOC v. Wabash National Corp.*, No. 5:24-cv-00148-BJB (W.D. Ky.)), after it said it first tried to resolve the matter without a lawsuit. When the agency [filed suit](https://www.eeoc.gov/newsroom/eeoc-sues-wabash-national-pregnancy-discrimination), EEOC trial attorney Kathleen Bensberg said, "No one should be forced to choose between their livelihood and the health of their pregnancy."

## What does the settlement require?

Under a two-year consent decree, Wabash will pay $50,000, according to the EEOC. The company will also:

- update its policies at all of its facilities nationwide;
- notify employees about their rights under the PWFA;
- train supervisors, managers, and human resources staff; and
- send the EEOC compliance reports on how its Cadiz facility responds to pregnancy-related accommodation requests.

Trade publication [Trailer Body Builders](https://www.trailer-bodybuilders.com/trailers/news/55419017/wabash-settles-eeoc-pregnancy-discrimination-lawsuit) and the Lafayette, Indiana, [Journal & Courier](https://www.yahoo.com/news/us/articles/wabash-national-settles-landmark-pregnancy-100112377.html) reported the same terms. Wabash is headquartered in Lafayette.

None of the announcements or news reports we reviewed included a statement from Wabash.

## Why does this case matter?

The PWFA took effect in June 2023, and this was the first lawsuit the EEOC brought to enforce it. Deputy EEOC General Counsel Christopher Lage said in the settlement announcement that "pregnant women are entitled to reasonable accommodations when needed to enable them to keep working safely."

The settlement comes as the EEOC prepares to narrow its PWFA rules, according to news reports we covered last week in [EEOC Plans to Narrow Pregnant Workers Fairness Act Rules](/articles/eeoc-pregnant-workers-fairness-act-rule-rollback/). The Wabash case didn't turn on the conditions that may be cut from the rule. It was about a basic accommodation during pregnancy, and the EEOC is still pursuing those.

## What this means for California workers

The situation the EEOC described, a pregnant worker sent home on unpaid leave after she asked to be moved, is one California law speaks to directly.

Federal law says a covered employer can't require you to take leave, paid or unpaid, if another reasonable accommodation can be provided (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg-1(4)). California's regulations go further. An employer with five or more employees can't require you to take a leave of absence because of pregnancy when you haven't requested leave (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, §§ 11035(h), 11039(a)(1)(H)).

California also lets you ask for a temporary transfer to a less strenuous or hazardous position (§ 11035(v)), and an employer can't deny a reasonable accommodation you request on your health care provider's advice (§ 11040(a)). Your employer may ask for medical certification to support the request (§ 11040(c)), so a note from your provider is worth getting early.

If your job has a task that doesn't work during pregnancy, put your request in writing, attach your provider's note, and suggest what would help: a different station, lighter duty, or a temporary transfer. Our guide on [how to ask for a pregnancy accommodation](/articles/how-to-ask-for-pregnancy-accommodation-california/) includes a sample request. If your employer answers by sending you home unpaid, keep the paperwork and talk to an employment lawyer before you resign.
