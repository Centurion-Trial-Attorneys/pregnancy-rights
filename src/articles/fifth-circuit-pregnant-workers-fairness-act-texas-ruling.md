A federal appeals court ruled on Friday, November 20, that Congress violated the Constitution when it passed the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act (PWFA), the 2022 law that requires employers to make reasonable accommodations for pregnant workers. The full U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit split 9-8, according to the [Associated Press, as published by PBS NewsHour](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/nation/appeals-court-says-federal-law-requiring-accommodations-for-pregnant-workers-is-unconstitutional), [Law Dork](https://www.lawdork.com/p/fifth-circuit-en-banc-quorum-clause-proxy-rule), and [The National News Desk](https://foxsanantonio.com/news/nation-world/pregnant-workers-ruling-puts-17-trillion-covid-era-house-vote-under-scrutiny). The ruling does not reach California, and California's own pregnancy accommodation law is unaffected.

## What did the court decide?

The case is about how the law was passed, not what it says. Congress approved the PWFA in December 2022 as part of a $1.7 trillion spending package. At the time, the House was using a COVID-era rule that let members vote by proxy, and most members weren't physically in the chamber, the AP reported.

Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton sued in 2023, arguing that the House lacked a quorum under the Constitution's Quorum Clause. The Fifth Circuit majority agreed. "'Proxy presence' does not count," the majority wrote, according to the AP.

Judge Cory Wilson wrote the majority opinion, Law Dork reported. Judge James Graves wrote the main dissent, joined by six other judges, calling the decision "illogical, inexplicable, and invalid." Judge Catharina Haynes dissented separately. The dissent also pointed out that Texas sued after "having accepted the vast majority of the funds that Congress appropriated to it," the AP reported.

## How did the case get here?

In February 2024, a federal district judge in Lubbock sided with Texas and permanently barred federal officials from enforcing the PWFA "against the State of Texas and its divisions and agencies," according to the [court's opinion](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/sites/default/files/images/press/Quorum%20Clause%20opinion.pdf). In August 2025, a three-judge Fifth Circuit panel reversed that decision, concluding in an [opinion by Judge Graves](https://www.ca5.uscourts.gov/Opinions/pub/24/24-10386-CV0.pdf) that the Quorum Clause "does not require physical presence." Judge Wilson dissented from that panel ruling. The full court then reheard the case, and Friday's decision reinstated the win for Texas.

## Who does the ruling cover?

That depends on whom you ask, and it may take more litigation to settle.

The order itself is narrow. The court did not void the whole spending package nationwide and said its relief is limited to this case, The National News Desk reported. Law Dork reported that the ruling "only addresses the enforceability" of the PWFA in Texas. The 2024 order it revived blocks federal enforcement against Texas as an employer, meaning its state agencies.

Advocates read the decision more broadly. Fifth Circuit rulings set binding law for federal courts in Texas, Louisiana, and Mississippi. The worker advocacy group A Better Balance, which pushed for the PWFA's passage, [said the ruling](https://www.abetterbalance.org/5th-circuit-invalidates-pregnant-workers-fairness-act-for-20-million-women/) "effectively erases essential rights to pregnancy accommodations for workers" in those three states, home to about 20 million women. Its president, Inimai Chettiar, called the decision "legally unsound and short-sighted," the AP reported.

The group noted that workers in those states still have other federal protections, including the Pregnancy Discrimination Act, the Americans with Disabilities Act, the Family and Medical Leave Act, and the PUMP Act for nursing workers.

## What happens next?

A Better Balance said it expects the case to go to the U.S. Supreme Court. The Justice Department, which defended the law, did not respond to the AP's request for comment. Law Dork noted the ruling could also invite challenges to other parts of the same 2022 spending law.

The decision adds to the pressure on the PWFA from Washington. The EEOC has signaled it plans to narrow the rules that explain the law, as we covered in our report on the [proposed PWFA rule rollback](/articles/eeoc-pregnant-workers-fairness-act-rule-rollback/).

## What this means for California workers

The Fifth Circuit's ruling doesn't bind courts in California. The PWFA was not struck down here.

More important, California didn't wait for Congress. Under the Fair Employment and Housing Act, it's unlawful for an employer to refuse a reasonable accommodation for a condition related to pregnancy, childbirth, or a related medical condition when you ask for it "with the advice of the employee's health care provider" (Gov. Code § 12945(a)(3)(A)). Your employer must engage in a good faith interactive process with you to find one (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11040(a)(2)(B)). And if you're disabled by pregnancy, you can take up to four months of pregnancy disability leave and return to work (Gov. Code § 12945(a)(1)). These rules apply to employers with five or more employees, and to the state and local governments regardless of size (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11035(h)).

That means a California worker who needs a change in duties, a reduced schedule, or another adjustment because of pregnancy has a state-law right to ask, even if federal protection weakens. If you asked for an accommodation and got a no, a runaround, or a sudden schedule cut, write down what you asked for, when, and what you were told, and keep copies somewhere other than a work account.
