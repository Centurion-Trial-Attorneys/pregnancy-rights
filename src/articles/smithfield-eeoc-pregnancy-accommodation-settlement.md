Smithfield Fresh Meats Corp. has agreed to settle a federal lawsuit in which the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) alleges the company refused to accommodate a pregnant worker's medical restrictions, put her on unpaid leave, and then fired her. The parties told the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of North Carolina about the deal in an October 13 filing, [according to Meatingplace](https://meatingplace.com/smithfield-reaches-settlement-in-pregnancy-discrimination-lawsuit/) and [Law360](https://www.law360.com/articles/2537744/smithfield-settles-eeoc-suit-over-pregnant-worker-s-firing). The terms have not been made public.

The case is one of the EEOC's early lawsuits under the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act (PWFA), the federal law that took effect in June 2023.

## What did the EEOC allege?

The EEOC filed the case in August 2025 (*EEOC v. Smithfield Fresh Meats Corp.*, No. 7:25-cv-01410-M). According to the [EEOC's announcement](https://www.eeoc.gov/newsroom/eeoc-sues-smithfield-fresh-meats-corp-pregnancy-discrimination), the worker told the company she was pregnant soon after she was hired at its Tar Heel, North Carolina, facility. After a workplace accident, she developed pregnancy complications that needed medical care. Her doctor set restrictions, including a lifting restriction, and she asked for an accommodation.

The EEOC alleges the company told her it does not provide accommodations for pregnancy. According to the complaint, as reported by Meatingplace, the company told her she could either return to work without restrictions or reapply after giving birth. She was placed on unpaid leave on September 8, 2023, and fired on September 27, 2023, the complaint alleges.

The EEOC says that conduct violated the PWFA and Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964. The agency said it tried to resolve the matter through its conciliation process before suing. It asked the court for back pay, compensatory and punitive damages, and policy changes to prevent pregnancy discrimination.

When the suit was filed, Melinda C. Dugas, the regional attorney for the EEOC's Charlotte District, said that "the law prohibits employers from forcing employees to take unpaid leave."

## What did Smithfield say?

The coverage of the settlement did not include a response from Smithfield to the allegations, and the EEOC's claims were never tested at trial. Meatingplace reports the settlement would resolve all claims in the case, but it still depends on the parties finalizing a proposed consent decree and filing it with the court.

## Why does this case matter?

According to the complaint, the worker asked for help about three months after the PWFA took effect. The law requires covered employers to make reasonable accommodations for known limitations related to pregnancy, childbirth, or related medical conditions unless the accommodation would cause an undue hardship (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg-1(1)). It also bars employers from requiring a worker to take leave, paid or unpaid, if another reasonable accommodation would work (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg-1(4)), and from taking adverse action against a worker for asking for or using an accommodation (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg-1(5)).

A blanket "we don't accommodate pregnancy" response, which is what the EEOC alleges here, is the kind of practice the PWFA was written to end. The case is a reminder that the EEOC is willing to go to court over it.

## What this means for California workers

The PWFA applies in California to employers with 15 or more employees. California law reaches further, covering employers with five or more.

Under California's pregnancy regulations, your employer can't refuse a reasonable accommodation when your health care provider advises that it's medically advisable (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11040(a)(1); Gov. Code § 12945(a)(3)(A)). You and your employer must work through the request together in a good-faith interactive process (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11040(a)(2)(B)). Accommodations can include a change in duties, job restructuring, or a reduced or intermittent schedule (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11040(b)). And firing someone because of pregnancy is unlawful (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11039(a)(1)(D)).

If you need an accommodation:

- **Put the request in writing** and attach your doctor's note.
- **Save every response.** If anyone tells you the company "doesn't do" pregnancy accommodations, write down who said it and when.
- **Watch the deadlines.** You generally have three years to file with the California Civil Rights Department, and 300 days to file a charge with the EEOC if your employer has 15 or more employees.
