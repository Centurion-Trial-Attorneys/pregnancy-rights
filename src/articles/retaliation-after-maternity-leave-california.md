**The short version:** No. California makes it unlawful to fire, discipline, or otherwise punish you because you took CFRA leave, and its pregnancy regulations bar retaliation for taking pregnancy disability leave (Gov. Code § 12945.2(k); Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11039(a)(2)(F)). You also have a right to return to your same job, or in limited cases a comparable one (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, §§ 11043(a), 11089(a)).
</div>

You come back from leave and something has shifted. Your biggest accounts went to the person who covered for you. Your manager suddenly has a list of performance problems nobody mentioned before you left. A write-up lands in your first month back. Or the job is just gone. Sometimes that's a coincidence. When it isn't, California law gives you real tools.

## What counts as maternity leave under California law?

California doesn't have a single law called maternity leave. Most new mothers use two different rights:

- **Pregnancy disability leave (PDL):** up to four months for the time you're disabled by pregnancy, childbirth, or a related medical condition (Gov. Code § 12945(a)(1)).
- **California Family Rights Act (CFRA) leave:** up to 12 workweeks in a 12-month period, which new parents use to bond with a baby. It applies if your employer has five or more employees and you have more than 12 months of service and at least 1,250 hours in the past year (Gov. Code § 12945.2(a), (b)(4)(A)).

Our guide to [how PDL and CFRA bonding leave stack](/articles/pregnancy-disability-leave-vs-cfra-bonding-leave/) walks through the timeline. The protections below apply to both.

## What does retaliation after maternity leave look like?

Under CFRA, your employer can't "refuse to hire, or to discharge, fine, suspend, expel, or discriminate against" you because you exercised your right to leave (Gov. Code § 12945.2(k)(1)). It also can't interfere with, restrain, or deny your leave rights (§ 12945.2(q)).

For PDL, the statute bars interference with your leave rights (Gov. Code § 12945(a)(4)). California's regulations spell out that this includes "retaliating against the employee because she has exercised her right" to take pregnancy disability leave, ask for an accommodation, or transfer (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11039(a)(2)(F)).

Retaliation rarely announces itself. It can look like:

- getting fired or "laid off" within weeks of coming back;
- a demotion, a pay cut, or fewer hours or shifts;
- a sudden bad review or write-up after years of good ones;
- losing your clients, projects, or territory to someone else;
- being left out of meetings and decisions you used to run; or
- comments about your "commitment" now that you have a baby.

## Do I have a right to get my old job back?

Yes, with narrow exceptions. After PDL, you're "guaranteed a right to return to the same position," and your employer must put that guarantee in writing if you ask (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11043(a)). Your employer can refuse your same position only if it proves you wouldn't have been in that job anyway, for legitimate business reasons unrelated to your leave, such as a layoff from a plant closure (§ 11043(c)(1)).

After CFRA leave, you're entitled to the same or a comparable position, which means one that is "virtually identical" in pay, benefits, shift, schedule, location, and working conditions, "including privileges, perquisites, and status" (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11089(b)). The regulation adds that you're entitled to reinstatement "even if the employee has been replaced or the employee's position has been restructured to accommodate the employee's absence" (§ 11089(a)(2)(A)).

So "we hired someone while you were out" doesn't end the conversation. The employer carries the burden of proving you wouldn't have been employed on your return date anyway (§ 11089(d)(1)).

## What if my employer says it was a layoff?

Real layoffs happen, and the regulations account for them. If you're laid off for legitimate reasons that have nothing to do with your leave, the employer's duty to reinstate you can end (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, §§ 11043(c)(3), 11089(d)(1)(A)). The employer has to prove it, though. Ask yourself:

- Were you the only one laid off, or one of a few new parents?
- Was your job eliminated, or retitled and handed to the person who covered for you?
- Did the "restructuring" start while you were on leave?
- Did the company post a similar job soon after letting you go?

If the answers point back to your leave, that's worth a closer look.

## What if I asked for an accommodation or complained?

You're protected there too. California's Fair Employment and Housing Act makes it unlawful to fire or discriminate against anyone because they opposed a practice the law forbids or filed a complaint (Gov. Code § 12940(h); Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11039(a)(1)(I)). It's also unlawful to fire you because of pregnancy (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11039(a)(1)(D)).

Federal law adds a layer. The Pregnant Workers Fairness Act bars adverse action against you for "requesting or using a reasonable accommodation" for limitations related to pregnancy, childbirth, or related medical conditions (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg-1(5)). It also bars retaliation for opposing a violation or filing a charge (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg-2(f)(1)).

## How do I show it was retaliation?

Most retaliation cases are built from three things:

- **Timing.** How soon after your leave, or your request, did things change?
- **Your track record.** Reviews, raises, and praise from before your leave make a sudden "performance problem" harder to believe.
- **How others were treated.** Did coworkers who didn't take leave keep their accounts, hours, or jobs?

Start collecting now. Save copies of old reviews, your leave approval, emails about your return, schedules, and pay stubs somewhere other than a work account. Write down dates and what people said while it's fresh.

## How long do I have to act?

You generally have three years from the unlawful act to file a complaint with the California Civil Rights Department (Gov. Code § 12960(e)(5)). That's the outside limit. Evidence fades and witnesses move on, so don't wait. If you've already been let go, our guide on [what to do in the first week after being fired while pregnant](/articles/fired-while-pregnant-first-week/) has steps that apply to new parents too.

## The takeaway

Taking the leave the law gives you is not a reason to lose your job, your pay, or your standing. If things changed when you came back, write down what happened, keep your records, and talk to an employment lawyer before you sign anything or resign.
