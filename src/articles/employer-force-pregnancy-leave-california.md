**The short version:** Generally, no. California's pregnancy regulations make it unlawful for an employer to require you to take a leave of absence because of pregnancy when you haven't asked for leave (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11039(a)(1)(H)). Federal law adds that an employer can't force you onto leave, paid or unpaid, if another reasonable accommodation would work (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg-1(4)).
</div>

It usually starts with a comment that sounds kind. "We think you should take it easy." "Maybe it's time to start your leave." Then your name comes off the schedule. Pregnancy leave is a right you choose to use. Your employer doesn't get to pick the date for you.

## What does California law say about forced pregnancy leave?

California's Civil Rights Department regulations list what employers can't do to pregnant workers. One item on that list: requiring an employee to take a leave of absence because of pregnancy or perceived pregnancy when she hasn't requested leave (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11039(a)(1)(H)). The rule covers employers with five or more full- or part-time employees (§ 11035(h)).

Notice the words "perceived pregnancy." Your employer can't push you out because it assumes you're pregnant, or assumes what your pregnancy means for your work.

The same list bars an employer from transferring you to another position over your objection because you're pregnant (§ 11039(a)(1)(G)). An employer can still move people for legitimate operational needs that have nothing to do with pregnancy, but "you're pregnant, so you're off the floor" doesn't qualify.

These rules have narrow exceptions, called permissible defenses: a bona fide occupational qualification, business necessity, or a practice required by law (§ 11039(b)). An employer's general worry about liability, or its sense that pregnant women "shouldn't" do certain work, is a different thing. If your employer claims a defense, that's a question to put in front of an employment lawyer.

## Who decides whether I'm too pregnant to work?

Your health care provider does. Under California's regulations, you're "disabled by pregnancy" if, in your health care provider's opinion, pregnancy makes you unable to perform one or more of the essential functions of your job, or unable to perform them without undue risk to yourself, your pregnancy, or other people (§ 11035(f)).

Pregnancy disability leave (PDL) covers the time you're actually disabled by pregnancy, up to four months (§ 11042(a)). It's tied to your provider's advice and your notice of the need for leave (§ 11042(c)). Your employer can ask for medical certification when you request leave or an accommodation (§§ 11040(c), 11042(c)). Nothing in those rules lets a manager decide on their own that your pregnancy makes you unfit and send you home.

## What does the federal Pregnant Workers Fairness Act add?

The federal Pregnant Workers Fairness Act (PWFA) lists several things covered employers can't do. Three matter here. An employer can't:

- require you to take leave, whether paid or unpaid, if another reasonable accommodation can be provided for your pregnancy-related limitations (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg-1(4));
- require you to accept an accommodation other than one worked out through the interactive process (§ 2000gg-1(2)); or
- take adverse action against you for requesting or using a reasonable accommodation (§ 2000gg-1(5)).

Under the PWFA, leave comes only after other accommodations have been ruled out. Our guide to [the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act in California](/articles/pregnant-workers-fairness-act-california/) explains how the federal law works alongside state law.

## What should my employer offer instead of leave?

If you have pregnancy-related limitations, the usual answer is a reasonable accommodation. California defines that as any change in the work environment or the way a job is customarily done that helps you perform the essential functions of your job (§ 11035(s)). Examples include changed duties, a modified schedule, more frequent breaks, a stool or other equipment, and break time with a private place to pump (§ 11035(s)(1)-(5)).

Your employer can't deny a reasonable accommodation you request on your provider's advice, and the two of you must work through a good-faith interactive process (§ 11040(a)). You can also ask for a temporary transfer to a less strenuous or hazardous position (§ 11035(v)). The key word is *ask*. You make the request. For a step-by-step walkthrough and a sample letter, see [how to ask for a pregnancy accommodation](/articles/how-to-ask-for-pregnancy-accommodation-california/).

## Can forced leave cost me later?

It can. You get up to four months of PDL per pregnancy, not per year. For a full-time employee working 40 hours a week, that's 693 hours (§ 11042(a)(1)). An employer that pushes you out early may try to count those weeks against your four months, leaving you less time when you need it most, around the birth.

Money is part of it too. Employers don't have to pay you during PDL unless they pay for other temporary disability leaves (§ 11044(a)), and an employer may require you to use accrued sick leave during PDL (§ 11044(b)(1)). An early, unwanted leave can drain sick time you were saving.

California's bonding leave under the California Family Rights Act (CFRA) is a separate right from PDL (§ 11046(a)). Our guide to [how PDL and CFRA bonding leave stack](/articles/pregnancy-disability-leave-vs-cfra-bonding-leave/) explains the full timeline.

## Is it retaliation if they push me out after I ask for help?

It can be. California makes it unlawful to deny, interfere with, or restrain your rights to reasonable accommodation, transfer, or PDL, including by retaliating against you because you used those rights (§ 11039(a)(2)(F)). If you asked for a stool or lighter duties and got a forced leave instead, write down the timeline.

## What should I do if my employer is pushing me onto leave?

- **Say no in writing.** A short email works: "I have not requested leave. I am able to work and would like to keep working." Keep a copy somewhere other than your work account.
- **Get your provider's input.** If you can work, ask your provider for a note saying so and listing any restrictions. If you need changes, ask for those in writing as an accommodation.
- **Ask what the concern is.** Make your employer name the specific job duty it's worried about. Then propose an accommodation that addresses it.
- **Keep a record.** Dates, who said what, schedule changes, and any emails or texts about your pregnancy.
- **Know where to file.** The Civil Rights Department takes complaints when pregnancy accommodations or leave are improperly denied ([CRD's Pregnant Employee notice](https://calcivilrights.ca.gov/wp-content/uploads/sites/32/2023/01/Your-Rights-and-Obligations-as-a-Pregnant-Employee_ENG.pdf)). Deadlines apply, so don't wait.

## The takeaway

Your leave is yours to request. If you can do your job, with or without an accommodation, your employer generally can't make you stop because you're pregnant. Put your objection in writing, offer a workable accommodation, and get help if your employer won't budge.
