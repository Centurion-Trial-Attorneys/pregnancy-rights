**The short version:** If you were fired while pregnant, use the first week to protect yourself. Write down what happened, check your final paycheck, ask for your personnel and payroll records in writing, and apply for unemployment or disability benefits. Don't sign a severance or separation agreement until you understand what you're giving up. You generally have three years to file a complaint with the California Civil Rights Department, but evidence fades much faster than that.
</div>

Losing your job while pregnant hits on every front at once: money, health insurance, and the baby on the way. You don't have to solve all of it this week. A few steps now can make a real difference later, whether you decide to challenge the firing or simply move on.

## Is it illegal to fire me because I'm pregnant?

Yes. California's Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA) bans sex discrimination, and "sex" includes pregnancy, childbirth, breastfeeding, and related medical conditions (Gov. Code §§ 12940(a), 12926(r)(1)). California's pregnancy regulations make it unlawful to fire someone because of pregnancy or perceived pregnancy, and unlawful to retaliate against an employee for using pregnancy rights (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11039(a)(1)(D), (I)). These rules cover employers with five or more employees.

Federal law adds protection at larger employers. The Pregnant Workers Fairness Act bars employers with 15 or more employees from taking adverse action against you because you asked for or used a pregnancy accommodation (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg-1(5)).

Employers rarely say pregnancy was the reason. They say "performance," "restructuring," or "not a good fit." What matters is the evidence, and the first week is when it's easiest to gather. For the bigger picture, see our guide to [your pregnancy rights at work in California](/articles/pregnancy-rights-at-work-california/).

## What should I write down right away?

Start a timeline today, while your memory is fresh. Include:

- When you told your employer you were pregnant, and who you told.
- Any comments about your pregnancy, your leave plans, or your "commitment" to the job.
- Every accommodation or leave request you made, and how your employer responded.
- What changed after you announced: new write-ups, cut hours, lost assignments, a cooler tone from your manager.
- Exactly what was said when you were fired, who was in the room, and the reason you were given.

Date the timeline and keep it on a personal device or in a personal account, not on a work laptop or in work email. Save the texts, emails, and performance reviews you already have. Don't download confidential company files.

## When do I get my final paycheck?

If you're fired, your employer must pay all the wages you've earned, including accrued vacation, immediately at the time of termination (Lab. Code § 201). If an employer willfully fails to pay on time, it can owe a waiting time penalty equal to your daily rate of pay for each day the wages stay unpaid, up to 30 days (Lab. Code § 203).

Check your last check against your hours, any overtime, and your unused vacation or PTO. If something is missing, raise it in writing and keep a copy.

## How do I get my personnel file and pay records?

Ask in writing. Email works, and it gives you a timestamped record of the request.

- **Personnel file:** Current and former employees have the right to inspect and get a copy of their personnel records. Your employer must respond within 30 calendar days of receiving your written request (Lab. Code § 1198.5).
- **Payroll records:** You can inspect or copy your payroll records, and your employer must comply within 21 calendar days of your request (Lab. Code § 226(b)).
- **Anything you signed:** On request, your employer must give you a copy of any document you signed relating to getting or keeping your job (Lab. Code § 432).

These records often show how your employer described your work before it learned about your pregnancy, and how that changed after.

## Can I get unemployment while pregnant?

Often, yes. To collect unemployment insurance, you must be unemployed through no fault of your own, physically able and available to work, and looking for work each week, according to the Employment Development Department (EDD). Pregnancy alone doesn't disqualify you. EDD's benefit guidelines say a pregnant claimant who is willing to work without restrictions is presumed available for work.

If your doctor says you can't work because of your pregnancy, you may qualify for State Disability Insurance instead. EDD says pregnancy disability benefits can start four weeks before your expected delivery date and last up to six weeks after delivery, or up to eight weeks after a cesarean. You can't receive unemployment and disability benefits for the same days, so if you switch, tell EDD to stop your unemployment payments first.

Don't wait on either application. The sooner you file, the sooner money starts coming in.

## Should I sign the severance agreement?

Not this week, and not until you understand it. Most severance agreements include a release: you give up your right to sue in exchange for the payment. If you were fired because of your pregnancy, that right has value, and a first offer doesn't always reflect it. Read every page, ask questions in writing, and have an employment lawyer review the agreement before you sign.

## What happens to my health insurance?

Ask HR in writing exactly when your coverage ends and what options you have to continue it. Watch your mail and email for continuation notices, and note any deadlines in them. If you're in the middle of prenatal care, tell your providers about the change so you aren't surprised by a bill.

## How long do I have to file a complaint?

Under California law, you must file with the Civil Rights Department (CRD) within three years of the date you were last harmed (Gov. Code § 12960(e)(5)). In employment cases, you need a Right-to-Sue notice from CRD before you can file a FEHA lawsuit in court. If your employer has 15 or more employees, you can also file a federal charge with the EEOC, which gives you 300 days from the date of the harm in California.

Three years sounds like a long time. With a newborn, it goes fast, and witnesses move on and texts disappear long before the deadline.

## Your first-week checklist

- **Write your timeline** and store it somewhere personal.
- **Save what you have:** texts, emails, reviews, and doctor's notes you gave your employer.
- **Check your final paycheck,** including unused vacation.
- **Request your personnel file, payroll records, and signed documents** in writing.
- **Apply for unemployment or disability benefits** with EDD.
- **Hold off on signing anything** until a lawyer has looked at it.
