<div class="tldr" markdown="1">
**The short version:** Yes. California requires every employer to give you a reasonable amount of break time to pump each time you need to, plus a private place to do it that is not a bathroom (Lab. Code §§ 1030, 1031). Federal law adds similar protections for one year after your child's birth (29 U.S.C. § 218d), and denying these rights can cost an employer premium pay and penalties.
</div>

You should not have to pump in a supply closet, a car, or a bathroom stall. California's lactation law covers private employers, the state, and local governments (Lab. Code § 1030). For the bigger picture on leave and accommodations, see our guide to [pregnancy rights at work in California](/articles/pregnancy-rights-at-work-california/).

## How much break time do I get to pump?

Your employer must give you "a reasonable amount of break time" to express breast milk for your infant child each time you need to (Lab. Code § 1030). The law does not cap you at a set number of breaks or minutes.

If possible, your lactation break should run at the same time as the rest breaks you already get (Lab. Code § 1030).

There is one narrow exception. An employer does not have to provide break time if doing so would "seriously disrupt" its operations (Lab. Code § 1032). Note the word "seriously."

## Are lactation breaks paid?

If your pumping break overlaps with a paid rest break you already get, that time is paid. Lactation time beyond your normal rest breaks can be unpaid (Lab. Code § 1030).

Under federal law, pumping time can only go unpaid if you are completely relieved from duty. If your boss expects you to answer emails while you pump, that time must be paid (29 U.S.C. § 218d(b)).

## Where am I allowed to pump?

Your employer must provide a room or other location for you to pump in private. It cannot be a bathroom (Lab. Code § 1031). The space must be:

- Close to your work area
- Shielded from view and free from intrusion
- Safe, clean, and free of hazardous materials
- Equipped with a surface for your pump and personal items, a place to sit, and access to electricity (or an alternative power source like an extension cord or charging station)

Your employer must also give you access to a sink with running water and a refrigerator to store milk, close to your workspace. If a refrigerator isn't feasible, a cooler or similar device can substitute (Lab. Code § 1031).

Your regular workspace can count if it meets every requirement (Lab. Code § 1031). The Labor Commissioner's guidance also says your employer cannot require a doctor's note or other documentation of your need to pump ([Labor Commissioner, Lactation Accommodation](https://www.dir.ca.gov/dlse/Lactation_Accommodation.htm)).

## What if I work for a small employer?

Employers with fewer than 50 employees can be exempt from a space requirement, but only if they show it would cause undue hardship (significant difficulty or expense given the size and resources of the business). Even then, they must still make reasonable efforts to give you a private place to pump that is not a toilet stall (Lab. Code § 1031).

That exemption applies only to the space rules. Your right to break time still stands. Separate rules apply to agricultural employers, shared buildings, and multiemployer worksites (Lab. Code § 1031).

## Does my employer need a written lactation policy?

Yes. Every California employer must have a lactation accommodation policy that explains your right to request accommodation, how to make the request, the employer's duty to respond, and your right to file a complaint with the Labor Commissioner (Lab. Code § 1034).

The policy must be in the employee handbook (or similar policies), given to new hires, and given to anyone who asks about or requests parental leave. If your employer can't provide break time or a compliant location, it must tell you in writing (Lab. Code § 1034).

## What does federal law add?

The federal PUMP for Nursing Mothers Act also requires reasonable break time and a private, non-bathroom place to pump, shielded from view and free from intrusion by coworkers and the public. These federal rights last for one year after your child's birth (29 U.S.C. § 218d(a)).

Since the PUMP Act took effect in December 2022, the federal rules cover nearly all employees protected by the Fair Labor Standards Act, including salaried employees who are exempt from overtime ([U.S. Department of Labor, Fact Sheet #73](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/73-flsa-break-time-nursing-mothers)). Employers with fewer than 50 employees can avoid the federal requirements only by showing undue hardship (29 U.S.C. § 218d(c)).

One procedural trap: before suing over a lack of private space, you generally must notify your employer and give it 10 days to fix the problem. That notice isn't required if you were fired for requesting break time or space or for objecting to the violation, or if your employer has said it won't provide a space (29 U.S.C. § 218d(g)).

Federal law does not override California's stronger protections (29 U.S.C. § 218d(h)).

## What happens if my employer denies me a break or a space?

You have options under California law:

- **Premium pay.** Denying reasonable break time or adequate space to pump counts as a rest period violation under Labor Code section 226.7 (Lab. Code § 1033(a)). According to the Labor Commissioner, that means you can recover one hour of pay at your regular rate for each violation ([Labor Commissioner, Lactation Accommodation](https://www.dir.ca.gov/dlse/Lactation_Accommodation.htm)).
- **Civil penalties.** You can report violations to the Labor Commissioner. If it finds a violation, it can issue a citation with a civil penalty of $100 for each day you were denied break time or adequate space (Lab. Code § 1033(c)).
- **Retaliation protection.** Your employer cannot fire or retaliate against you for using or trying to use these rights. You can file a retaliation complaint with the Labor Commissioner (Lab. Code § 1033(b)), and the Labor Commissioner says to file within one year of the retaliation.

Lactation is also protected under California's Fair Employment and Housing Act. The definition of "sex" in FEHA includes breastfeeding and medical conditions related to breastfeeding (Gov. Code § 12926(r)(1)(C)). Being treated worse or pushed out because you're nursing can be unlawful sex discrimination.

## What should I do now?

Ask for what you need in writing, even in a short email, and ask HR for a copy of the lactation policy. If you get a no, a bathroom, or silence, write down the date and time of every denied break and every conversation about it. Save any written response. Those records matter if you file a wage claim, a Labor Commissioner complaint, or a discrimination claim. Deadlines apply, so don't wait.
