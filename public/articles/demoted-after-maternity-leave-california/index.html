Can My Employer Demote Me After Maternity Leave in California?

California law guarantees your same or a comparable job after pregnancy and bonding leave. Here's what counts as a demotion and when restructuring is legal.

Guide · By Ryan Stygar, Employment Attorney · November 23, 2026

The short version: Generally, no. After pregnancy disability leave you have a right to your same job, and after CFRA bonding leave you're entitled to your same job or one that is "virtually identical" in pay, benefits, duties, status, location, and schedule (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, §§ 11043(a), 11089(b)). A new title with less responsibility, a smaller territory, or a worse shift can break that promise, and "we restructured while you were out" is not a defense on its own (§ 11089(a)(2)(A)).

You come back from leave and your title is the same, but your team reports to someone else and your biggest clients are gone. Or HR offers a "similar role" with a schedule that doesn't work with child care. California has specific rules for this, and they're stricter than many employers assume.

What job am I entitled to come back to?

Most new mothers in California take pregnancy disability leave (PDL), then California Family Rights Act (CFRA) leave to bond with the baby. Our guide to how PDL and CFRA bonding leave stack explains the timeline.

After PDL, you're "guaranteed a right to return to the same position," and your employer must put that guarantee in writing if you ask (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11043(a)). The "same position" is the job you held before your leave, accommodation, or transfer (§ 11035(i)). PDL covers employers with five or more employees, plus the state and local governments regardless of size (§ 11035(h)).

After CFRA leave, your employer must reinstate you to "the same or a comparable position" (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11089(a)(1)). Refusing to do that is an unlawful practice unless one of a few narrow defenses applies (§ 11089(a)(2)).

What makes a job "comparable"?

This is where most demotion cases turn. A comparable position isn't just any job at the same pay. Under both the PDL and CFRA rules, it must be "virtually identical" to your old job in pay, benefits, and working conditions, "including privileges, perquisites, and status" (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, §§ 11035(j), 11087(i)). The regulations add that it must:

  • involve "the same or substantially similar duties and responsibilities," with "substantially equivalent skill, effort, responsibility, and authority";
  • be at the same or a "geographically proximate" worksite; and
  • ordinarily have the same shift or the same or an equivalent schedule.

California's CFRA definition says "comparable position" has the same meaning as "equivalent position" under the federal Family and Medical Leave Act (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11087(i)). The federal rules add a useful detail: equivalent pay "includes any bonus or payment, whether it is discretionary or non-discretionary" (29 C.F.R. § 825.215(c)(2)). They also say the standard doesn't reach "de minimis, intangible, or unmeasurable aspects of the job" (§ 825.215(f)). Losing authority, accounts, or bonus eligibility is different.

What does a demotion after leave look like?

It's rarely called a demotion. Watch for changes like these:

  • your title stays, but your direct reports, budget, or sign-off authority move to someone else;
  • your sales territory, book of business, or key clients go to the person who covered for you;
  • your shift or schedule changes in a way you didn't ask for;
  • you lose eligibility for a bonus, commission plan, or premium pay; or
  • you're offered a "new role" with a lower grade, a smaller scope, or a different reporting line.

Any one of these can mean your job isn't "virtually identical." Several together make the case stronger.

Can my employer say the job was restructured while I was gone?

Not as an automatic excuse. The CFRA regulations say you're entitled to reinstatement "even if the employee has been replaced or the employee's position has been restructured to accommodate the employee's absence" (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11089(a)(2)(A)). If the company reshuffled your duties because you were out, it has to put you back.

What your employer can argue is that you'd have lost the job or duties anyway, for reasons that have nothing to do with your leave. You have "no greater right to reinstatement" than if you'd been working the whole time, and the employer must prove by a preponderance of the evidence that you wouldn't otherwise have been employed in that job on your return date (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, §§ 11043(c)(1), 11089(d)(1)). The PDL regulation gives a layoff from a plant closure as an example (§ 11043(c)(1)). Under CFRA, if a shift was eliminated or overtime was cut, you don't get that shift or those hours back (§ 11089(d)(1)(B)).

Ask yourself: Did the reorganization start after you left? Did it touch anyone else? Did the person who covered for you keep your duties?

What if there's no comparable job open?

After PDL, if your employer proves you wouldn't have kept your same position anyway, it still owes you a comparable one unless it proves one of two things: it wouldn't have offered you a comparable position even if you'd been at work all along, or no comparable position is available (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11043(c)(2)). A position counts as available if it's open on your scheduled return date or within 60 calendar days and you're qualified for it, and your employer has an affirmative duty to tell you about open positions (§ 11043(c)(2)(B)1., 2.).

If you lost a license or certification because of your leave, you must be given "a reasonable opportunity" to meet that requirement after you return, rather than being moved down for it (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11089(a)(2)(B)).

Is a demotion after maternity leave also pregnancy discrimination?

It can be. California's Fair Employment and Housing Act makes it unlawful to discriminate against you "in compensation or in terms, conditions, or privileges of employment" because of sex (Gov. Code § 12940(a)), and "sex" includes pregnancy, childbirth, breastfeeding, and related medical conditions (Gov. Code § 12926(r)(1)). Employers also can't "interfere with, restrain, or deny" your pregnancy leave rights (Gov. Code § 12945(a)(4)).

If the demotion came with a sudden write-up or comments about your commitment now that you're a parent, read our guide on retaliation after maternity leave. The two problems often show up together.

What can I agree to?

You can choose a different role. Your employer may grant your own request for a different shift, schedule, position, or location, or offer you a promotion (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11089(b)(2)). A change you asked for is different from one you were pressured into. If HR presents a new role as your only option, ask in writing what happened to your old job.

The takeaway

Before you return, ask your employer in writing to confirm you're going back to your same position. Once you're back, compare title, pay, bonus plan, duties, reports, clients, location, and schedule with what you had. Write down every difference and save the emails that explain them somewhere other than a work account. The California Civil Rights Department's quick guide to pregnancy and bonding leave confirms the same-or-comparable job rule. Complaint deadlines apply, so if your job shrank while you were gone, talk to an employment lawyer before you accept the change or resign.
