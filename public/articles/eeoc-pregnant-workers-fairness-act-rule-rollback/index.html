EEOC Plans to Narrow Pregnant Workers Fairness Act Rules, Reports Say

The EEOC plans to propose narrower Pregnant Workers Fairness Act rules as soon as this month. Here's what could change and what California law still covers.

In the news · By Danielle Rini, Litigation Counsel · November 9, 2026

The U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) is preparing to narrow the federal rules that tell employers which pregnancy-related conditions they must accommodate, according to reporting by The New York Times (republished by The Philadelphia Inquirer) and HR Dive. Nothing has changed yet. The current rules stay in place until a new one is final, and the public gets a chance to comment first.

What did the EEOC announce?

In a regulatory agenda filing, the EEOC said it plans to revise its regulations under the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act (PWFA) sometime in November, HR Dive reported on November 3. The agency said it intends to revise its interpretation of the phrase "pregnancy, childbirth or related medical conditions," which decides which conditions qualify for accommodation. HR Dive reported that the EEOC "has not specified what changes it will make" and that the comment period is expected to end sometime in January.

The Times, citing seven people with knowledge of the discussions, reported that the EEOC "plans to publish the proposed narrowing of conditions eligible for accommodations as soon as this month."

What could be cut?

The PWFA requires covered employers to make reasonable accommodations for workers' known limitations related to pregnancy, childbirth, or related medical conditions. The EEOC's current rule, finalized in 2024, gives a long, non-exhaustive list of what that covers. It includes fertility treatment, the use of contraception, miscarriage, stillbirth, abortion, lactation, and menstruation (29 C.F.R. § 1636.3(b)).

According to the Times' sources, the EEOC could cut that list of "related medical conditions" and leave the question to employers and courts. Wider religious exemptions are also under consideration, the Times reported. Its sources said EEOC Chair Andrea Lucas is not expected to eliminate the requirements that most directly protect women during pregnancy, but could eliminate explicit protections for conditions that affect women who are not pregnant.

Lucas criticized the 2024 rule when the commission adopted it. "The rule fundamentally errs in conflating pregnancy and childbirth accommodation with accommodation of the female sex, that is, female biology and reproduction," she said in 2024, as quoted by the Times. HR Dive quoted her 2024 statement that the rule "opens the door to requiring accommodations potentially extending to a myriad of conditions ranging from infertility to menstruation to hormone issues to menopause."

Part of the rule has already been struck down. In May 2025, a federal judge in Louisiana vacated the portion requiring employers to accommodate elective abortions, in a case brought by Tennessee and 16 other Republican-led states. According to HR Dive, that ruling did not affect abortions stemming from treatment of a medical condition related to pregnancy.

What did the EEOC and its critics say?

An EEOC spokesperson told HR Dive that the agency does not comment on potential rulemaking or speculate about future regulatory actions. The spokesperson said that since the start of the second Trump administration, the agency "has taken more than 40 public enforcement actions involving pregnancy-related discrimination," including 24 lawsuits, and obtained $1.6 million in relief. A spokesperson told the Times that Lucas "remains deeply committed to enforcing the laws that protect pregnant workers."

Worker advocates warned the change would push employers into medical judgments. "It's going to put employers in the position of making medical decisions about the reproductive health of their workers," Gillian Thomas of the ACLU Women's Rights Project told the Times.

What happens next?

If the EEOC publishes a proposed rule in the Federal Register, anyone can file comments before the agency finalizes it. Until then, the 2024 rule remains in effect, minus the abortion provisions the Louisiana court vacated. Expect lawsuits once a final rule comes out. What this means for California workers

A federal rollback would not change California's own pregnancy protections. California's Fair Employment and Housing Act regulations require employers to provide a reasonable accommodation when your health care provider advises that one is medically advisable (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11040(a)). Those regulations define pregnancy-related conditions to include lactation (§ 11035(d)) and define "related medical condition" to include loss or end of pregnancy and recovery from it, along with conditions such as gestational diabetes and preeclampsia (§ 11035(u)). Being "disabled by pregnancy" includes needing time off for prenatal care, severe morning sickness, and bed rest (§ 11035(f)).

California also gives up to four months of pregnancy disability leave at employers with five or more employees, according to the Civil Rights Department. None of that depends on the EEOC.

Where the federal change could matter is coverage California's regulations don't spell out the same way, such as fertility treatment, contraception, or menstruation. If you need an accommodation for one of those, ask in writing, include your provider's note, and keep copies. If your employer says no and points to the federal rules, that's a good moment to talk to an employment lawyer about whether California law still covers you.
