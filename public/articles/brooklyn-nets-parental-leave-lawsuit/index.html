Former Brooklyn Nets Executive Sues, Saying He Was Fired After Taking Parental Leave

A former Brooklyn Nets vice president alleges in a new federal lawsuit that the team fired him after he took leave to care for his newborn son.

In the news · By Ryan Stygar, Employment Attorney · October 26, 2026

A former vice president of cybersecurity for the Brooklyn Nets has sued the NBA team, alleging it fired him because he took parental leave after the birth of his son. Ryan Whalen filed the lawsuit on October 21 in federal court in the Eastern District of New York, according to Bloomberg Law and HR Dive. The case is Whalen v. Brooklyn Nets, LLC, No. 1:26-cv-06771, according to the court docket.

The team denies the claims. None of the allegations have been proven in court.

What does the lawsuit allege?

Whalen joined the Nets in 2024, according to Bloomberg Law. According to HR Dive's account of the complaint, he took two periods of leave under the federal Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) and New York's Paid Family Leave program to care for his newborn.

Before his leave, Whalen says, he received two performance reviews showing he was meeting expectations. While he was out, the complaint alleges, the team removed him from the list of employees scheduled to attend an NBA conference "without prior discussion with him and without explanation."

When he came back, Whalen alleges, a supervisor "received him with uncharacteristic hostility" and repeatedly refused to speak with him. He then received what he calls a "sudden" negative review and was fired 16 days after returning to work, HR Dive reports. He alleges the firing was unlawful retaliation for taking protected leave. The docket lists the case as a Family and Medical Leave Act lawsuit.

How did the Nets respond?

Marissa Shorenstein, chief operating officer of Brooklyn Sports & Entertainment, the Nets' parent business, denied the claims in an email to HR Dive. "BSE is committed to a workplace free from discrimination," she wrote.

The team has not publicly explained the negative review or the firing.

Why does this case matter?

Parental-leave retaliation is often talked about as a problem mothers face. This lawsuit is a reminder that fathers and other non-birthing parents take leave too, and the law protects them when they do.

The sequence Whalen describes will sound familiar to many workers: solid reviews before leave, a cold reception on return, then a sudden bad review and a firing. Timing like that is often central to a retaliation claim. An employer can still defend itself by showing it had real, documented performance concerns that had nothing to do with the leave, and the Nets will have the chance to do that here.

What this means for California workers

If you work in California, you have strong bonding-leave rights, and they don't depend on your gender or whether you gave birth.

The California Family Rights Act (CFRA) gives eligible employees up to 12 workweeks of job-protected leave to bond with a new child (Gov. Code § 12945.2(a)). CFRA covers employers with five or more employees (Gov. Code § 12945.2(b)(4)(A)), far fewer than the 50 employees the federal FMLA requires (U.S. Department of Labor). To qualify, you generally need more than 12 months with your employer and at least 1,250 hours worked in the year before your leave (Gov. Code § 12945.2(a)).

California's regulations say your employer "cannot use the taking of CFRA leave as a negative factor in employment actions," such as hiring, promotions, or discipline (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11094(b)). Discouraging you from taking leave you're entitled to is illegal interference (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11094(a)).

You may also be able to get paid while you're out. California's Paid Family Leave program pays up to eight weeks of benefits to eligible workers who bond with a new child, according to the Employment Development Department. Paid Family Leave replaces part of your wages; CFRA is what protects your job.

If you're worried about what happens when you come back:

  • Save your reviews. Keep copies of performance reviews and positive feedback from before your leave, outside of work systems.
  • Write down changes. Note the date and details of anything that shifts when you return, like lost assignments, a cold shoulder, or new criticism.
  • Watch the timing. A sudden bad review or a "restructuring" right after leave is worth a closer look.
