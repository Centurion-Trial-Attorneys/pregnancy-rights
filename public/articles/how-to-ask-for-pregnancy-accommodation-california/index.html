How Do I Ask for a Pregnancy Accommodation at Work in California?

How to ask your California employer for a pregnancy accommodation, what your doctor's note needs to say, deadlines, and a sample request.

Guide · By Ryan Stygar, Employment Attorney · October 21, 2026

The short version: Tell your employer what you need, in plain words, and put it in writing. In California, an employer with five or more employees can't refuse a reasonable pregnancy accommodation your health care provider says is medically advisable, and it must respond within 10 calendar days. A short email and a simple note from your provider are usually enough. A sample request is below.

Asking for help at work while you're pregnant can feel like a risk. It shouldn't. California law gives you the right to ask, sets rules for how your employer has to answer, and makes it illegal to punish you for asking. Here's how to make the request so it's clear, documented, and hard to ignore.

What counts as a pregnancy accommodation?

An accommodation is any change to your work environment or the way your job is usually done that helps you keep doing the essential parts of your job. California's regulations list examples, including modified duties, schedule changes for earlier or later hours or more frequent breaks (for example, to use the restroom), a stool or chair, modified equipment, and break time and a private space to pump (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11035(s)).

The list isn't exhaustive. Common requests include a lifting limit, more water and snack breaks, time off for prenatal appointments, a temporary shift change, or less time on your feet. You can also ask for a temporary transfer to a less strenuous or less hazardous position if your provider advises it (Gov. Code § 12945(a)(3)(C)).

Do I need to use special legal words?

No. California only requires notice that is "sufficient to make the employer aware" that you need an accommodation, and it can be oral or written (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11050(a)(1)). You don't have to cite a statute.

The same is true under the federal Pregnant Workers Fairness Act (PWFA), which covers employers with 15 or more employees (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg(2)(B)(i)). The EEOC's guidance says you can use plain language, you don't have to mention the PWFA, and you don't have to say the words "reasonable accommodation" (29 C.F.R. pt. 1636, app. A).

Even so, put your request in writing. A conversation is legally valid, but an email gives you a dated record of what you asked for and when.

When should I ask?

If you can see the need coming, California asks you to give at least 30 days' notice before the accommodation starts. If that isn't possible, give notice as soon as practicable (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11050(a)(2), (a)(3)). If the need is sudden, ask right away.

What does my doctor's note need to say?

Your employer may ask for a medical certification, but it doesn't have to (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11040(c)). If it does, the note is sufficient when it covers three things (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11050(b)(6)):

  • A description of the accommodation or transfer you need.
  • A statement that it is medically advisable because of your pregnancy.
  • The date the need started (or will start) and how long it's expected to last.

Your diagnosis isn't on that list. Once a note meets these requirements, your employer must accept it and can't demand more information than the regulations allow (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11050(b)(8)). Your employer must also keep any medical information you provide confidential as required by law (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11050(b)(9)).

If your employer asks for certification, it has to give you at least 15 calendar days to provide it (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11050(b)(2)). The note can come from your doctor or from other providers who treat your pregnancy, such as a nurse practitioner, nurse midwife, or licensed midwife (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11035(n)).

How fast does my employer have to respond?

California requires your employer to respond as soon as practicable, and no later than 10 calendar days after it receives your request (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11050(a)(5)).

You and your employer must then work through the request in a good-faith interactive process (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11040(a)(2)(B)). That means a real back-and-forth. If your employer proposes something different, it should explain why, and you can respond. Under the PWFA, an employer can't force you to accept an accommodation other than one reached through that process, and it can't push you onto leave if another accommodation would let you keep working (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg-1(2), (4)).

Can I use a sample request?

Yes. Adjust this to fit your job and your provider's advice, then send it to your manager and HR by email:

Subject: Pregnancy accommodation request

Hi [Name],

I'm pregnant, and my health care provider has advised that I need the following changes at work: [for example, no lifting over 20 pounds and a 10-minute break every two hours]. I expect to need these starting [date] through [date or "the end of my pregnancy"].

I'm requesting these as a pregnancy accommodation. I've attached a note from my provider. [Or: I can provide a note from my provider if you need one.]

I want to keep doing my job, and I'm happy to talk about how to make this work. Please let me know your response in writing.

Thank you, [Your name]

Keep it short and factual. You don't need to share your medical history, and you don't need to apologize.

What if my employer says no or stalls?

Ask for the reason in writing. Whether an accommodation is reasonable is decided case by case, considering things like your medical needs, how long you'll need the accommodation, and how your employer has handled similar requests in the past (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11040(a)(2)(A)).

Asking for an accommodation is also protected. California makes it illegal for an employer to interfere with or deny your pregnancy accommodation rights (Gov. Code § 12945(a)(4)), and the PWFA bars punishing you for requesting or using one (42 U.S.C. § 2000gg-1(5)). If your hours get cut, your reviews suddenly turn negative, or you're let go after you ask, read our guide on what to do if you're fired while pregnant.

Using an accommodation doesn't use up your pregnancy disability leave, with one exception: if your accommodation is a reduced schedule or intermittent time off, those hours can count against your leave (Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11040(b)).

Is California changing these rules?

Possibly. On October 19, 2026, California's Civil Rights Council released proposed updates to these regulations, including adding telework as an example of an accommodation and expanding which providers can support a request (Civil Rights Department). They are only proposals for now. The current rules described above still apply.

The bottom line

Ask early, ask in writing, and keep copies of everything: your request, your provider's note, and every reply. A clear, documented request is the best protection you have, and it puts the burden where it belongs, on your employer to respond.
