Your rights, from the first trimester to the first year

California and federal law protect you at every stage. These are the rights employers get wrong most often.

  • No discrimination

    Your employer can't fire you, demote you, cut your hours, or pass you over because you're pregnant or just had a baby.

    Pregnancy rights at work

  • Reasonable accommodations

    More breaks, a seat, lighter duties, or a temporary transfer when your health care provider advises it.

    Accommodation questions

  • Job-protected leave

    Up to four months of pregnancy disability leave, then up to 12 weeks of bonding leave if you qualify, with your job waiting.

    How leave works

  • Pumping breaks and a private space

    Break time to pump whenever you need it, and a private place to do it that is never a bathroom.

    Lactation breaks

  • Leave after a loss

    Up to five days off after a miscarriage, stillbirth, failed adoption, failed surrogacy, or unsuccessful assisted reproduction.

    Reproductive loss leave

  • No retaliation

    Asking for an accommodation, taking leave, or complaining can't cost you your job. Punishment for it is illegal.

The numbers California employers should know

Most of these rights apply once your employer has five employees.

  • 4

    Months of pregnancy leave

    Up to four months of job-protected leave while you're disabled by pregnancy or childbirth, with health coverage continued.

    Gov. Code § 12945(a)(1), (a)(2)(A)
  • 12

    Weeks of bonding leave

    Up to 12 workweeks to bond with a new child if you've worked there more than a year and 1,250 hours.

    Gov. Code § 12945.2(a)
  • 5

    Days after a loss

    Up to five days of leave after a miscarriage, stillbirth, or other reproductive loss.

    Gov. Code § 12945.6(b)(1)
  • $100

    A day for denied pumping time

    The Labor Commissioner can fine an employer for each day it denies break time or a proper space to pump.

    Lab. Code § 1033(c)

Questions working moms ask us

Can my employer fire me or treat me differently because I'm pregnant?

No. California's Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA) makes it illegal for an employer with five or more employees to fire you, refuse to hire you, demote you, cut your pay, or change your job because of your sex. Under FEHA, sex includes pregnancy, childbirth, breastfeeding, and related medical conditions (Gov. Code §§ 12926(d), 12926(r)(1), 12940(a)). If you were written up, pushed out, or passed over after you announced a pregnancy, the timing matters. Write down what happened and when.

How much pregnancy leave do I get in California?

If pregnancy, childbirth, or a related condition disables you, you can take up to four months of pregnancy disability leave (PDL) per pregnancy (about 17⅓ weeks for a full-time worker). PDL applies to employers with five or more employees, with no minimum time on the job (Gov. Code § 12945; Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, §§ 11035, 11037, 11042).

After PDL, the California Family Rights Act (CFRA) can add up to 12 weeks of bonding leave within a year of the birth. You need more than 12 months on the job and at least 1,250 hours worked in the 12 months before your leave (Gov. Code § 12945.2; Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, § 11090).

Does my employer have to accommodate my pregnancy?

Yes, if the request is reasonable and comes on your health care provider's advice. California requires employers with five or more employees to provide reasonable accommodations for pregnancy, such as modified duties, more breaks, or a seat. You can also ask to transfer to a less strenuous or hazardous position when your provider says it's medically advisable (Gov. Code § 12945; Cal. Code Regs., tit. 2, §§ 11040, 11041). Federal law adds protection: the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act covers employers with 15 or more employees and bars them from forcing you onto leave when another accommodation would work.

Do I get breaks to pump at work?

Yes. Every California employer must give you a reasonable amount of break time to express milk each time you need it (Lab. Code § 1030). When possible, that time runs during your regular paid rest breaks. Extra time beyond those breaks can be unpaid. Federal law also requires break time and a private space other than a bathroom for one year after your child's birth (29 U.S.C. § 218d). For the details, read our lactation guide.
